The short answer: every team needs one place that all model calls go
through, from the first day. Whether that place is a gateway product depends
on three triggers: a second model provider, a spend limit you must enforce, or
a fallback policy for outages. Before any of those appear, a thin internal
wrapper does the job with less to operate.

## What a gateway actually does

An AI gateway sits between your code and the model providers and exposes one
endpoint for all of them. What it adds is not the proxying; it is the rules
that apply to every call:

- **Keys and providers in one place**, so application code never holds a
  provider credential.
- **Retries and fallbacks**, including switching provider when one is down.
- **Budgets and rate limits** per team, feature or customer.
- **Caching**, including a [semantic cache](/glossary/semantic-cache) for
  repeated questions.
- **Logs and traces** of every request, response, model and cost.

[The gateway is the product](/blog/the-gateway-is-the-product) argues that
these decisions are where much of the real engineering in an LLM system
lives. That is true. It does not follow that you need to buy or deploy a
gateway to make them on day one.

## When a thin wrapper is enough

If all of these hold, a function in your codebase that every call goes
through is the right size:

- You call **one provider**, through its official SDK.
- **One service** makes model calls, so there is nowhere for policy to drift.
- You have **no per-customer or per-team spend limits** to enforce in real
  time.
- An outage of that provider is **acceptable downtime**, not an incident.

Make the wrapper do three things: attach an identity to every call, log
model, tokens, latency and cost, and keep the provider-specific code in one
file. That is most of the value of a gateway, and it is an afternoon of work.
When the triggers below arrive, you replace the inside of one function rather
than every call site.

## The triggers that mean you need one

**A second provider.** The moment two providers are in play, request and
response shapes, error codes and rate-limit behaviour diverge. A gateway
normalises them. Writing that normalisation yourself is how teams end up
maintaining a worse gateway.

**A spend limit that must hold.** A budget is only real where it is enforced
before the call, not in a report afterwards. When finance or a customer
contract requires a cap, it belongs in the request path, once.

**An outage that is an incident.** Failing over to another provider or model
needs health tracking, a [circuit breaker](/glossary/circuit-breaker) and
retries with jitter. These are fiddly to get right and easy to get wrong
under pressure.

**Several services calling models.** Once three teams each call providers
directly, keys, logging and limits drift apart. A gateway is the cheapest way
to get them back into one place.

## Self-hosted or managed

A self-hosted gateway such as LiteLLM keeps traffic and logs inside your
network and avoids a third party in the request path. A managed one removes
the operations work and often bundles analytics. The trade is the same one as
anywhere else in the stack: control and data residency against the cost of
running another service. The [LLM gateway comparison](/compare/llm-gateways)
lays out the options and when to skip each.

## What not to do

Do not route between models on price before you can measure quality. A
cheaper model that fails your eval set is not cheaper, and a gateway makes
that mistake very easy to deploy at scale. Build the
[eval set first](/compare/gateway-guardrails-evals), then let the gateway
route on what it shows.
