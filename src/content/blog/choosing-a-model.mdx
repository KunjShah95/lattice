Every few months a new model tops a benchmark, and every few months a team
reorganises around it. Then six months later they are paying more, getting
roughly the same quality, and unable to say why.

The underlying mistake is treating model choice as a selection problem. You
pick one, you wire it in, you are done. It is a *routing* problem, because
your traffic is not one task — it is a distribution of tasks with different
difficulty, different latency requirements and different tolerances for error.

## The escalation trap

The intuitive policy is: start cheap, escalate to a big model if the answer
looks bad. It is wrong more often than it works, for three reasons.

**You cannot tell whether the answer is bad.** The model that produced the
answer is a poor judge of its own output, and a cheap model judging a big
model's work is worse still. Without an external signal — a validator, a
schema check, a downstream test — "looks bad" is a vibe.

**Failures cluster on hard cases.** The requests a small model gets wrong are
not random; they are the genuinely hard ones. So escalation does not rescue the
weak case, it just pays twice for it. You have saved nothing on the easy 90% —
you already served those — and you have *doubled* the cost of the hard 10%.

**The retry loop is where the spend goes.** A policy that escalates on failure
is one provider hiccup away from a retry storm against your most expensive
model, with your most expensive model, on the requests most likely to time out.

## Route on something you can observe

Policies that work route on signals that exist before the model is called:

**Explicit task identity.** If your system has named operations — classify,
extract, summarise, rewrite, reason — route on the operation. This is the
cleanest signal available and most teams already have it implicitly in their
code structure. It requires no classifier and no guessing.

**User or tenant tier.** A paying customer and a free trial are different
quality bars. This is blunt but it is honest, and it is trivially enforceable.

**Deterministic input properties.** Length, whether a schema is required,
whether the input is code, whether it contains a retrieval result. All of these
are knowable before inference and correlate with difficulty more reliably than
anything you can infer from the text itself.

**An explicit difficulty classifier.** A small model that scores input
difficulty before you route. This works, and it is the right structure — the
point is that the classifier makes an up-front decision rather than judging
output after the fact. RouteLLM and commercial routers are built this way.

What you should *not* route on is a subjective read of the prompt. "This
sounds hard" is where the escalation trap starts.

## Keep the decision in one place

Wherever the routing decision is made, it needs three properties:

**It is measurable.** A routing policy you cannot evaluate is a guess with
extra steps. Each route needs its own quality number on a
[shared eval set](/blog/evals-are-the-asset), so that moving a boundary
between routes is a decision you can check rather than a change you hope for.

**It has a fallback.** Per-provider, per-model circuit breakers, with jitter on
retries so a partial outage does not become a total one by stampeding the
survivor. Provider error rates are real and they are not independent.

**It degrades rather than fails.** When the big model is unavailable, the
correct behaviour is usually the small model with a caveat, not a 500.

## Escape hatches, and their cost

Two legitimate reasons to escalate on failure exist, and both are narrow:

- **Validated output.** If a schema or test gates the result, a failure is
  *observable* and a retry is *justified*. This is the exception that proves
  the rule — it works because you have an external signal. [Structured
  output](/blog/prompt-or-finetune) is what makes this cheap.
- **Human in the loop.** Where a person sees the failure, escalation is a UX
  decision rather than an optimisation, and cost is much less sensitive.

What does not work is judging quality with the same class of model that
produced it, in the same call, at the same price.

## Keep the provider behind an interface, not behind a hard choice

The real insurance is that swapping models should not require touching
application code. That means a [gateway](/blog/the-gateway-is-the-product) in
front from day one, even if it initially does nothing but log requests and map
model IDs.

The cost of that day-one indirection is a few hours. The cost of not having it
is that every provider deprecation becomes an incident, and you will pay that
bill within the first year.

## Where to start

1. Enumerate the distinct operations in your codebase. You probably have five
   to eight, not one.
2. Assign each to the cheapest model that clears your quality bar on a real
   eval set.
3. Put a gateway in front and move the routing decision into it.
4. Measure cost per successful outcome, not cost per request — see
   [the cost model](/blog/the-cost-model).
